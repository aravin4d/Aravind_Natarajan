Make the call

Release REL-13, three days before go-live. The readiness score is 54, and red: three Sev2 bugs open, regression at 90.8% against a 95% target, a third of the sign-offs in, and three QA days left of the six it needs.

You have three moves. Try them, and watch the score, the checklist and the call change.

The score uses the workbook’s own weights: open bugs 30%, regression 20%, UAT and sign-offs 15%, time and scope 15%, late changes 10% and defect trend 10%. Green from 80, amber from 60.

Your moves

Score 54, red. No-go. Three Sev2 bugs are open, QA has half the days it needs, and three changes landed late.

What I’d recommend

Slip three days and fix the oldest Sev2 bugs first. Pushing the late changes out on its own doesn’t get the release out of red. I don’t stop a release: I show what each choice buys, in points and in days, and let the owner decide with the numbers in front of them.

Small decisions that show how I manage

Check the data before the numbers

Twelve data-health checks run first, and each one says which tab to fix.

Numbers that become actions

Sixteen rules turn the numbers into today’s to-do list, each with its reason and a link.

Goals in the open

All 97 KPIs have a plain-English meaning, and the 44 that need a target carry a Good and a Watch goal that colour the tiles.

Scores that get checked

The release history compares each readiness score with what happened next: a clean release, a hotfix, a rollback or an incident.

People are not a leaderboard

Role IDs only. Pulse scores stay anonymous and show only with three or more answers, and 1:1 notes live in a manager-only section.

Runs anywhere

No macros or add-ins, Excel 2016 or later. Paste the ClickUp export, and the weekly routine takes about ten minutes.

where I learned to lead

The lead who showed me how

Over my years at Amazon I reported to a local manager and a skip-level manager, and on the Identity team I worked under Kenny Herrington, a software lead based in Boston. He was the first person who showed me what leading a team really looks like. He took real care of all of us: he taught us, gave us room to make mistakes and learn from them, and helped with anything we needed. It was the steepest learning of my career. With his guidance I grew into a senior role, and then into leading a team of more than ten. Most of how I lead today started with him.

AssociateSenior AssociateLeading a team of 10+

Every team since has shaped me too. At Brightcove I learned a lot under my manager, Bob Barber, whose focus was always his team’s health and growth. But Kenny is where it started.

She joined straight out of college. I taught her everything I knew at the time: how to test, how to own a feature, how to talk to stakeholders. In under two years she was leading features and projects under me. When she got curious about work outside QA, I pointed her to the right resources and the right people. He was our QA Lead in title, but mostly tested whatever came to him. He already knew how to test better than most. What held him back was communication and business thinking. how I hire

Tools can be learned. The way someone thinks takes much longer. I’ve interviewed more than 40 candidates, and every one goes through the same loop and the same scorecard, so the decision is about the person, not the day.

  1. Their own workThey walk me through something they tested themselves, not what their team did: what they chose not to test, and why.
  2. A real requirementI give them a PRD and see how creative, and how risk-aware, their test scenarios get.
  3. A live productI open a real, public website, often a government one, because nobody has seen its test cases. They find API and UI mismatches, then tell me which ones would hurt a customer, and why.
  4. The write-upThey write up their best find as a bug report. A clear report tells me more about how someone communicates than any question.

I score each round straight after it, before I compare notes with anyone, and I’d rather wait for the right person than fill a seat.

What I look for, and how much it counts

  • Judgment and risk sense30%

    What they test first, what they’d skip, and why.

  • Customer thinking20%

    Whether they can say why a bug matters to a real person.

  • Communication20%

    A clear bug report, and a clear account of their own work.

  • Technical depth15%

    APIs, logs, data and tools, AI included.

  • Ownership and learning15%

    How they talk about their mistakes, and what changed after.

making quality everyone’s job

Quality works best when it isn’t only QA’s job. At Amazon I ran bug bashes often. At Buncha I ran two company-wide and five or six more by team and feature.

I also pull Support in. When the people who talk to customers every day understand the product deeply, fewer problems turn into escalations.
