how I hire

Tools can be learned. The way someone thinks takes much longer. So I take the first round myself and ask candidates to walk me through their own work, not what their team did. I give them a PRD and see how creative their test scenarios get.

In the live round I open a public government website and ask them to find API and UI mismatches. Then comes the part that matters most: why each one matters, whether it helps the customer, whether the site actually feels easy to use.

making quality everyone’s job

Quality works best when it isn’t only QA’s job. At Amazon I ran bug bashes often. At Buncha I ran one company wide and plenty of smaller ones by team and feature.

I also pull Support in. When the people who talk to customers every day understand the product deeply, fewer problems turn into escalations.

the summit

How I got here, a game built from my career, what I do off the clock, and a letter, sealed and addressed to you.

how I got here

I’ve always needed to know why things work the way they do.

As a kid I took things apart to see how they worked. As I got older, that turned into a habit of comparing everything I owned with the best version of it on the market, and working out exactly where mine fell short.

Then I got my first Google Nexus. It was buggy with nearly every update, and honestly, I loved it. I reported bugs to Google and signed up for every beta program I could find. Somewhere along the way I realised the interesting part wasn’t finding the bug. It was picturing the people who would run into it, and what it would cost them.

The other half started around exam time. Friends would gather around me before tests, and I’d explain a concept one way, then another, then with some unrelated example, until it clicked for each of them. Explaining one idea five different ways made me understand it better than any textbook did. It’s the same thing QA asks of you, seeing one product through many people’s eyes, and it’s why mentoring came naturally once I started leading.

That eye for detail got me into Amazon in 2017, straight out of an Electronics and Communication degree. At Brightcove I sharpened it. Our clients were broadcasters and enterprises in different countries, so before testing anything for a client, I researched them first: what their viewers expected, how they used the platform, and why. Then I tested standing in their shoes.

Along the way I kept solving problems instead of waiting for someone else to. My first move is always a simple fix that unblocks people today, then the long term fix, so the problem doesn’t come back. What I enjoy most is growing with a company while the people around me grow too. A process I build is only any good if the team can run it without me.

where I think QA is heading

I think the next few years will change QA more than the last twenty. AI will write and run most routine tests, and that’s good news, because it frees QA for the part no tool can own: judgment. Deciding what’s worth testing. Spotting the risk nobody wrote into a requirement. Knowing a feature isn’t ready even when every test passes. The QA teams that matter will sit beside product from the first conversation, own the customer’s experience end to end, and use AI to move faster instead of fearing it. That’s the team I want to build next.

Buncha · 2025 to 2026 · Built from zero

Building QA from zero

How I turned last minute testing at a startup into a release process the whole company could plan around.

01

What I walked into

When I joined Buncha, QA mostly lived in people’s heads. There was no written process, no shared idea of what tested meant, and QA usually saw a feature right before it went out. Releases went out as daily hotfixes. Everyone was working hard. The system just didn’t give anyone a chance to catch problems early.

02

How I looked at it

I stopped treating QA as a step at the end. It had to become the system that decides what’s ready to ship, with clear points where QA gets involved, all the way from the PRD to production.

03

What I put in place

Nothing ships without a signoff

Every release carries a signed QA signoff. Putting your name on a release changes how carefully you look at it.

An internal UAT before the real one

After QA is done, I run a full final pass myself. Only when it holds up does it move to external UAT.

Start a week early

PRDs get checked a week before the sprint. Test approach, test cases and acceptance criteria are agreed before development starts.

Clear owners, one severity scale

Team ownership, SDLC release gates and a P0 to P3 severity framework, so everyone knows who acts and how fast.

Every task leaves a trail

QA release notes for every task and a recorded video for every closure. The recordings ended up powering product demos too.

Write it all down

Ramp up guides for every tool we use daily, so a new person never depends on someone’s memory.

04

Protecting the team

About six months in, we lost a QA position and didn’t replace it. The work kept growing, people were getting worn out, and one engineer kept underestimating tasks and rushing the finish. First I covered the gap myself with a final UAT pass, so nothing broken reached customers.

Then I changed how we plan. I start from the release date and work backwards: when UAT signoff has to happen, how much buffer we need, and the estimate for every task, before anyone picks it up. I plan the team at roughly 60% for planned work and keep 40% for RCA, urgent issues, meetings and everything else.

Taming the SOS

Why every small issue was waking the whole company at night, and the framework that fixed it.

01

What I noticed

In my first weeks I kept seeing the same thing. Anything that went wrong after hours, even one user’s minor problem or something that could wait until morning, triggered an SOS call. Every senior engineer and manager got the call, and then they called their teams.

I watched it for two months before proposing anything, because I wanted to understand the pattern first.

02

How I looked at it

It wasn’t an engineering problem. It was an escalation problem. Most of those calls didn’t need an engineer at all. They needed someone to look at the issue and decide how serious it really was.

03

What I designed

P0 to P3

One severity scale everyone can use, with clear timelines for tasks, bugs and escalations at each level.

SOS means company wide

An SOS is only for something that breaks for everyone. Not one user, not a minor inconvenience, not something that can wait.

Support and SRE go first

They handle the first response. If it isn’t resolved within a fixed window, SRE pulls in QA or the engineer who owns that area.

The right person after hours

I also proposed a dedicated after hours SRE hire, so nights didn’t depend on whoever picked up the phone.

04

Getting people to use it

Support pushed back at first, which was fair, because it put more on their plate. I aligned with leadership that resolving customer concerns belongs with Support, then made sure they could do it. I trained them myself over five or six sessions on our products, and kept running a session every month on everything going live.

Once it was running, I handed ownership to Operations and SRE. I still review the exceptions, and adjust when something doesn’t follow the map.

Shaping the product before it’s built

The most valuable testing I do happens before a single line of code exists.

01

What I noticed

Buncha moved fast, and it had to. A lot of requirements were written to fix the problem in front of us that day. What often got missed were the edge cases that land on real people: a shopper finishing late, a driver on the road, a worker whose pay depends on a clock in record.

02

How I looked at it

If I only test what’s already built, all I can do is report problems. If I’m in the room when it’s designed, I can prevent them. So I started reading every requirement the way the people using it would live it.

03

Three places it mattered

Shift clock in and clock out (When I Work)

The ask was simple: clock in, and clock out automatically. With several shifts in a day, automatic can go wrong in a lot of ways. In my second month I wrote the complete requirements and every expected case myself:

  • Automatic clock out only after a person’s last shift of the day
  • Handle a last shift that runs long
  • Never clock out a driver mid drive, since the day’s last shift is almost always drivers
  • Alert dispatch when someone hasn’t clocked out, instead of making the worker ask for an extension
  • Block the app without a clock in, because pay is calculated from this data

It has scaled with the business ever since, without a single issue.

Working without a shift

A request to let people keep working in the grace period after their shift, with no shift on record. It looked like a convenience fix, but it could affect pay and create a compliance problem.

A popup for drivers

A request to make drivers tap a popup to confirm they were still working. Drivers shouldn’t be looking at their phones while driving, legally or for their own safety.

Both requests changed, but not quickly. It took several rounds of discussion, and I held my position because the risk was real.

Launching the core systems

For shopper auto assignment, capacity automation and multi store van routing, I was in the discussions from day one. I questioned the logic from the customer’s side, flagged what could go wrong, planned how the old system would keep running as a fallback, and worked out rollout and rollback. Use cases, acceptance criteria and test cases were ready while the design was still being discussed.

Coimbatore, India · 2026

To the team reading this,

I started in 2017, straight out of an Electronics and Communication degree, testing the Alexa app on a weekly release cycle. Close to ten years later I’ve been a founding QA member at Amazon, the only QA owner for an entire region at Brightcove, and the person who built QA from nothing at a Series A startup. The companies changed. The way I approach the work hasn’t.

What I do best. I find the gap nobody has been assigned yet, and I close it. Sometimes that’s a process that only lived in someone’s head, so I write it down and turn it into a standard. Sometimes it’s a dependency that keeps slowing everyone down, so I build the tool that removes it. I’d rather fix something at the root once than patch it every week.

How I work. I get involved long before work reaches QA and stay with it until it’s safely in production. I read requirements the way a customer will live them, and think through every realistic edge case, for the end user and for the people running the operation behind them. And I test production every day, because good judgment comes from knowing how a system actually behaves.

Small things and big things. The small things get the same care as the big ones: a clear bug report, an honest estimate, a new hire walked through every workflow on day one. On the big things, I’m clear about what’s right for the customer, and I make that case early, in planning, with evidence, so the decision is made together. I measure success by what the customer never has to deal with.

The leader I’m becoming. I lead the way I’d want to be led: clear standards, honest and specific feedback, and real investment in each person’s growth. I’m proudest when someone on my team no longer needs my help with something they used to. Quality isn’t a department. It’s a habit a whole company shares.

What I’m looking for. A QA Manager or Head of QA role in a business moving fast enough that quality is genuinely at stake, where QA sits beside product and shapes what ships. I want to grow the function and the people in it at the pace the company grows, and keep using AI to build tools the whole team benefits from.

If that sounds like your team, I’d love to talk.
