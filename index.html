how I hire

Tools can be learned. The way someone thinks takes much longer. I’ve interviewed more than 40 candidates, and every one goes through the same loop and the same scorecard, so the decision is about the person, not the day.

  1. Their own workThey walk me through something they tested themselves, not what their team did: what they chose not to test, and why.
  2. A real requirementI give them a PRD and see how creative, and how risk-aware, their test scenarios get.
  3. A live productI open a real, public website, often a government one, because nobody has seen its test cases. They find API and UI mismatches, then tell me which ones would hurt a customer, and why.
  4. The write-upThey write up their best find as a bug report. A clear report tells me more about how someone communicates than any question.

I score each round straight after it, before I compare notes with anyone, and I’d rather wait for the right person than fill a seat.

What I look for, and how much it counts

  • Judgment and risk sense30%

    What they test first, what they’d skip, and why.

  • Customer thinking20%

    Whether they can say why a bug matters to a real person.

  • Communication20%

    A clear bug report, and a clear account of their own work.

  • Technical depth15%

    APIs, logs, data and tools, AI included.

  • Ownership and learning15%

    How they talk about their mistakes, and what changed after.

making quality everyone’s job

Quality works best when it isn’t only QA’s job. At Amazon I ran bug bashes often. At Buncha I ran two company-wide and five or six more by team and feature.

I also pull Support in. When the people who talk to customers every day understand the product deeply, fewer problems turn into escalations.

how I got here

I’ve always needed to know why things work the way they do.

As a kid I took things apart to see how they worked. Later, I compared everything I owned with the best version of it on the market, and worked out exactly where mine fell short. When my first smartphone kept shipping buggy updates, I started reporting the bugs and joining every beta I could find.

Somewhere along the way I realised the interesting part wasn’t finding the bug. It was picturing the people who would run into it, and what it would cost them. I was also always the one who explained things, one idea five different ways until it clicked for everyone. That’s still how I coach.

That curiosity got me into Amazon in 2017, where Kenny Herrington showed me what leading a team looks like. At Brightcove I learned a lot under Bob Barber, and sharpened how I test: before testing anything for a client, I researched their market, then tested standing in their viewers’ shoes.

Along the way my job changed from finding problems to building the systems that prevent them, and the teams that run those systems. What I enjoy most is growing with a company while the people around me grow too. A process I build is only any good if the team can run it without me.

where I think QA is heading

I think the next few years will change QA more than the last twenty. AI will write and run most routine tests, and that’s good news, because it frees QA for the part no tool can own: judgment. Deciding what’s worth testing. Spotting the risk nobody wrote into a requirement. Knowing a feature isn’t ready even when every test passes. The QA teams that matter will sit beside product from the first conversation, own the customer’s experience end to end, and use AI to move faster instead of fearing it. That’s the team I want to build next.

Building QA from zero

How I turned last-minute testing at a startup into a release process the whole company could plan around.

01

What I walked into

When I joined Buncha, QA mostly lived in people’s heads. Fifteen developers were shipping two mobile apps and an admin web app, with no written process and no shared idea of what “tested” meant. QA usually saw a feature right before it went out, and hotfixes went out daily, often several times a day. Everyone was working hard. The system just didn’t give anyone a chance to catch problems early.

02

How I looked at it

QA couldn’t stay a step at the end. It had to become the system that decides what’s ready to ship, with clear points where QA gets involved, from the PRD to production.

03

What I put in place

Three gates before anything ships

A signed QA sign-off, then an internal UAT I ran with each feature’s product owner, then a final review by the leadership sign-off owner. Putting your name on a release changes how carefully you look at it.

One release day a week

Hotfixes moved into a single Wednesday window, with the full sprint release every two weeks, so everyone could plan around them.

Start a week early

PRDs reviewed a week before the sprint, with test approach, test cases and acceptance criteria agreed before development starts.

Clear owners, one severity scale

Team ownership, release gates and a P0 to P3 severity framework, so everyone knows who acts and how fast.

Every escape becomes a test

Every bug customers found, or that surfaced after release, was reviewed, traced to how it got through, and added to the regression suite.

Every task leaves a trail

QA release notes for every task and a recorded video for every closure. The recordings ended up powering product demos too.

Write it all down

Ramp-up guides for every tool we used daily, so a new person never depended on someone’s memory.

04

Shaping the product before it’s built

The most valuable testing I do happens before a single line of code exists.

01

What I noticed

Buncha moved fast, and it had to. A lot of requirements were written to fix the problem in front of us that day. What often got missed were the edge cases that land on real people: a shopper finishing late, a driver on the road, a worker whose pay depends on a clock-in record.

02

How I looked at it

If I only test what’s already built, all I can do is report problems. If I’m in the room when it’s designed, I can prevent them. So I started reading every requirement the way the people using it would live it.

03

Three places it mattered

Shift clock-in and clock-out (When I Work)

The ask was simple: clock in, and clock out automatically. With several shifts in a day, automatic can go wrong in a lot of ways. In my second month I wrote the complete requirements and every expected case myself:

  • Automatic clock-out only after a person’s last shift of the day
  • Handle a last shift that runs long
  • Never clock out a driver mid-drive, since the day’s last shift is almost always drivers
  • Alert dispatch when someone hasn’t clocked out, instead of making the worker ask for an extension
  • Block the app without a clock-in, because pay is calculated from this data

It has scaled with the business ever since, without a single issue.

Working without a shift

A request to let people keep working in the grace period after their shift, with no shift on record. It looked like a convenience fix, but it could affect pay and create a compliance problem.

A popup for drivers

A request to make drivers tap a popup to confirm they were still working. Drivers shouldn’t be looking at their phones while driving, legally or for their own safety.

Both requests changed, but not quickly. It took several rounds of discussion, and I held my position because the risk was real.

Launching the core systems

For shopper auto-assignment, capacity automation and multi-store van routing, I was in the discussions from day one. I questioned the logic from the customer’s side, flagged what could go wrong, planned how the old system would keep running as a fallback, and worked out rollout and rollback. Use cases, acceptance criteria and test cases were ready while the design was still being discussed.

We began negotiating scope during planning instead of at handover, and that’s how I ended up reviewing and signing off PRDs alongside their owners.

After our Head of Product left, I reported directly to the CEO and worked with him on both QA and product. For my last five months I was part of product too: shaping features, making product changes directly when the team was stretched, and aligning leadership when product was busy.
