About

I'm a QA leader who likes being in the room early. Most of the problems I've caught over the years were visible long before a line of code was written: in a requirement that missed how a driver actually works, or a process that only made sense to the person who wrote it. That's where I spend my time.

I've done this at three very different scales. At Amazon I was one of the founding QA members on Alexa Identity, which shipped to 18 locales. At Brightcove I was the only QA owner for the whole APAC region. At Buncha, a Series A grocery delivery startup, I built QA from nothing and ran it with a team of three.

What I enjoy most is growing with a company while the people around me grow too. A process I build is only any good if the team can run it without me.

How I got here

I've always needed to know why things work the way they do.

As a kid I took things apart to see how they worked. As I got older, that turned into a habit of comparing everything I owned with the best version of it on the market, and working out exactly where mine fell short.

Then I got my first Google Nexus. It was buggy with nearly every update, and honestly, I loved it. I started reporting bugs to Google and signed up for every beta program I could find, going through each new feature and pointing out where it didn't behave the way it should. At some point I realised the interesting part wasn't finding the bug. It was picturing the people who would run into it, and what it would cost them. That's still how I think about quality.

The other half of the story started around exam time. Friends would gather around me before tests, and I'd explain a concept one way, then another way, then with some completely unrelated example, until it clicked for each of them. Explaining one idea five different ways made me understand it better than any textbook did. It's exactly what QA asks of you, seeing one product through many different people's eyes, and it's why mentoring came naturally once I started leading teams.

That's become the core of how I test. I look at the same feature through different eyes, because each one finds different problems: someone using it for the very first time, a regular who's in a hurry, a demanding customer who expects it to just work, someone who doesn't even know what the product is for, the product manager who designed it, and the leadership team who has to answer for it. The details I catch are usually the ones that only show up from one of those seats.

That eye for detail is what got me into Amazon. At Brightcove I sharpened it. Our clients were broadcasters and enterprises in different countries, and what worked for one market didn't always make sense for another. So before testing anything for a client, I'd research them first: what their viewers expected, how they actually used the platform, and why. Then I tested it standing in their shoes.

Once I was in QA, I kept solving problems instead of waiting for someone else to. My first move is always a simple fix that unblocks people today. Then I go after the long-term fix, so the problem doesn't come back. That habit is why I build tools and processes, and why I care so much about root causes.

where I think QA is heading

I think the next few years will change QA more than the last twenty. AI will write and run most routine tests, and that’s good news, because it frees QA for the part no tool can own: judgment. Deciding what’s worth testing. Spotting the risk nobody wrote into a requirement. Knowing a feature isn’t ready even when every test passes. The QA teams that matter will sit beside product from the first conversation, own the customer’s experience end to end, and use AI to move faster instead of fearing it. That’s the team I want to build next.

I was retyping near-duplicate bug reports by hand. Bytecast records a web page and captures everything happening on it, then turns that recording into a bug in one click.

  • Built-in lightweight video editing and custom bug templates, or manual entry when there's nothing to record
  • Claude-powered, and can switch models to keep bug write-ups concise every time
  • Files into Jira, ClickUp, and five-plus other trackers out of the box
  • The real trick: every recorded video, console log, network call, and inspected element gets bundled into one interactive file attached to the bug. Open that attachment and it plays back like the live page, logs synced to the exact moment they happened
  • Cross-checks the details a reporter typed against what the logs actually show, so a bug report isn't just a story, it's backed by evidence

how I hire

Tools can be learned. The way someone thinks takes much longer. So my interviews are built around thinking. I take the first round myself and ask candidates to walk me through their own work, not what their team did. I give them a PRD and see how creative their test scenarios get.

In the live round I open a public government website and ask them to find API and UI mismatches. Then comes the part that matters most: why each one matters, whether it helps the customer, whether the site actually feels easy to use. I'm looking for people who speak for the user.

making quality everyone's job

Quality works best when it isn't only QA's job. At Amazon I ran bug bashes often. At Buncha I ran one company-wide and plenty of smaller ones by team and by feature.

I also pull Support in. When the people who talk to customers every day understand the product deeply, fewer problems turn into escalations.

I find the gap nobody has been assigned yet, and I close it. Sometimes that's a process that only ever lived in someone's head, so I write it down and turn it into a standard. I want to grow the function, and the people in it, at the pace the company grows, and to keep using AI to build tools the whole team benefits from, not just ways to test faster.

If that sounds like what your team needs, I'd love to talk.

Building QA from zero

How I turned last-minute testing at a startup into a release process the whole company could plan around.

01

What I walked into

When I joined Buncha, QA mostly lived in people's heads. There was no written process, no shared idea of what "tested" meant, and QA usually saw a feature right before it went out. Releases went out as daily hotfixes. Everyone was working hard. The system just didn't give anyone a chance to catch problems early.

02

How I looked at it

I stopped treating QA as a step at the end. It had to become the system that decides what's ready to ship, with clear points where QA gets involved, all the way from the PRD to production.

03

What I put in place

Nothing ships without a signoff

Every release carries a signed QA signoff. Putting your name on a release changes how carefully you look at it.

An internal UAT before the real one

After QA is done, I run a full final pass myself. Only when I'm satisfied does it move to external UAT. If it isn't there yet, it goes back.

Start a week early

PRDs get pre-validated a week before the sprint. Test approach, test cases and acceptance criteria are agreed before development starts.

Every task leaves a trail

QA release notes for every task, and a recorded video for every task closure. Those recordings ended up powering product demos too.

Write it all down

Ramp-up guides and practical instructions for every tool we use daily, so a new person never depends on someone's memory.

One release rhythm

I proposed and ran a one-release-a-week cadence to keep the team focused, until we moved to six-week sprints with interim releases.

04

Protecting the team

About six months in, we lost a QA position and didn't replace it. The work kept growing, people were getting worn out, and one of my engineers kept underestimating tasks and rushing the finish. First I covered the gap myself with a final UAT pass, so nothing broken reached customers.

Then I changed how we plan. I start from the release date and work backwards: when UAT signoff has to happen, how much buffer we need, and the estimate for every task, before anyone picks it up. I plan the team at roughly 60% for planned work and keep 40% for RCA, urgent issues, meetings and everything else. It gave the team room to breathe and made our dates believable.

Taming the SOS

Why every small issue was waking the whole company up at night, and the framework that fixed it.

01

What I noticed

In my first weeks I kept seeing the same thing. Anything that went wrong after hours, even one user's minor problem or something that could easily wait until morning, triggered an SOS call. Every senior engineer and manager got the call, and then they called their teams.

I was honestly surprised we needed everyone online for issues that small. I watched it for two months before proposing anything, because I wanted to understand the pattern first.

02

How I looked at it

It wasn't an engineering problem. It was an escalation problem. Most of those calls didn't need an engineer at all. They needed someone to look at the issue and decide how serious it really was.

03

What I designed

P0 to P3

One severity scale everyone could use, with clear timelines for tasks, bugs and escalations at each level.

SOS means company-wide

An SOS is only for something that breaks for everyone. Not one user, not a minor inconvenience, not something that can wait for the morning.

Support and SRE go first

They handle the first response. If it isn't resolved within a fixed window, SRE pulls in QA or the engineer who owns that area.

The right person after hours

I also proposed a dedicated after-hours SRE hire, so nights didn't depend on whoever happened to pick up the phone.

04

Getting people to use it

Support pushed back at first, which was fair, because it put more on their plate. I aligned with leadership that resolving customer concerns belongs with Support, and then made sure they could actually do it. I trained them myself over five or six sessions on our products, and kept running a session every month on everything going live.

Once it was running, I handed ownership to Operations and SRE, since they're the ones watching after hours. I still review the exceptions. When something doesn't follow the map, I work out whether a piece was missing or it was human error, and adjust.

Shaping the product before it's built

The most valuable testing I do happens before a single line of code exists.

01

What I noticed

Buncha moved fast, and it had to. A lot of requirements were written to fix the problem in front of us that day. What often got missed were the edge cases that land on real people: a shopper finishing late, a driver on the road, a worker whose pay depends on a clock-in record.

02

How I looked at it

If I only test what's already built, all I can do is report problems. If I'm in the room when it's designed, I can prevent them. So I started reading every requirement the way the people using it would live it.

03

Three places it mattered

Shift clock-in and clock-out (When I Work)

The ask was simple: clock in, and clock out automatically. But with several shifts in a day, "automatic" can go wrong in a lot of ways. In my second month I wrote the complete requirements and every expected case myself:

  • Auto clock-out only after a person's last shift of the day
  • Handle a last shift that runs long
  • Never clock out a driver mid-drive, since the last shift of the day is almost always drivers
  • Alert dispatch when someone hasn't clocked out, instead of making the worker ask for an extension
  • Block the app without a clock-in, because pay is calculated from this data

It has scaled with the business ever since, without a single issue.

Working without a shift

One request was to let people keep working during the grace period after their shift ended, without a shift being created. It looked like a convenience fix. In practice it meant people working with no shift on record, which can affect their pay and creates a compliance problem.

A popup for drivers

Another request asked drivers to tap a popup to confirm they were still working. Drivers shouldn't be looking at their phones while driving, legally or for their own safety, so we shouldn't ask them to.

Both requests changed, but not quickly. It took several rounds of discussion, and I held my position because the risk was real.

Launching the core systems

For shopper auto-assignment, capacity automation and multi-store van routing, I was in the discussions from day one. I questioned the logic from the customer's side and from a plain logical standpoint, which brought in changes that weren't in the original plan. I also flagged what could go wrong, planned how the old system would keep running as a fallback, and worked out the rollout and rollback strategy. Use cases, acceptance criteria and test cases were ready while the design was still being discussed.

04

The turning point

This didn't start smoothly. Early on, my questions came late in the cycle, after work had already reached QA, and they cost us time. Product and engineering weren't always happy with the back and forth, and I understood why.

So I moved my questions to the start. We began negotiating scope during planning instead of at handover, and that's how I ended up reviewing and signing off PRDs alongside their owners, instead of only testing what came out the other end.
